The Sunday Letter · Issue 11

The Person Nobody
Wants to Fight

Lauren Fine, MD November 15, 2026 6 min read

Good morning.

This week I added another tab to my mental load. Setting boundaries with family, friends and colleagues who always seem to push them. And we all know that the holidays bring this out in full force.

If you missed last week, here is the short version.

The mental load is the noticing, the remembering, the planning, the deciding. None of it is hard on its own. The weight comes from holding all of it at once.

For the next few weeks I am taking it apart, one piece at a time. Each Sunday gets one focus, one worksheet, and a couple of things worth your time. Last week was the invisible list.

This week: the fight you dread.

We all have that friend, family member, or colleague, who embodies the phrase “pushing boundaries”. If turned into a cartoon caricature or meme we would see them with arms crossed, brows furrowed, stomping until the whole group folds.

They always get their way. Nobody wants to deal with it. Including you.

When I see someone who usually sets boundaries, or calls me out when I don’t set them myself, blind (consciously or not) to their own situation, it hurts.

Many of you are physicians, moms, or both. You wouldn’t let your patients get away with this and we all know what happens when we give in to our kids' tantrums just that one time. They push back harder the next time you try to set a boundary and you have to work harder to stand your ground.

At work: It’s the colleague who complains loudly until the call schedule bends. The partner who never takes a holiday. The one who vetoes every change in the meeting until everyone stops proposing them.

In motherhood: It’s the parent who runs the class group chat. The family member who decides where and how the holiday happens. The co-parent who digs in until you drop it.

Both at once: All of this is manageable most of the time. But what happens when the holiday season causes all of this to collide, and the loudest person sets the terms for all of it?

Why physician moms give in

We de-escalate for a living. Angry patients, scared families. Keeping the peace is a clinical skill we bring home. But by the time we come home or pick up the kids, we are already running on empty. Giving in feels like triage, not weakness. We are used to being the one who absorbs it so everyone else can move on. But giving in now just creates a greater burden for ourselves, especially when giving in forces us to also give in on our values and someone else ends up suffering for it.

The real question

It isn't “give in or push back.” It's whether you are choosing to let it go, or avoiding the fight because you dread it. Both look the same from the outside. They feel very different inside. Avoiding the fight because we dread it just adds to our mental load. We hold onto it, doubt our decision and have to deal with the consequences. Letting it go allows us to, as Mel Robbins says, “let them,” and then we forget about it. But the latter only works if it doesn’t affect anyone else or your values. Avoiding the fight doesn't close the tab. It stays open.

What to do with it

Worksheet: Is this one worth it?

Preference or values? Which restaurant, which weekend, which side dish: let it go. Excluding someone, or a schedule that is unfair every single time: hold the line.

Who pays the price? If only you pay, it's yours to give. If your kids, your partner, or a colleague pays, it isn't really your energy you're saving.

One time or a pattern? One concession is a choice. A pattern teaches the tantrum works. Same as with a three year old.

How will I feel in two days? Fine means it was a good trade. Still stewing means you didn't save energy. You just moved where you spend it.

The coach's view

Choosing your battles is a skill. Letting something go is not weakness when it is a decision. It only costs you when it is a reflex.

Expect it to get worse before it gets better. Behavioral psychology calls this an extinction burst. When a tactic stops working, people push harder before they quit. If you ever sleep trained a baby, you know night two is worse than night one. Readers who expect the spike are less likely to cave at the peak.

Don't go first alone. In a group, or a department, everyone waits for someone else to say it. Line up one or two allies before the moment comes.

I am usually non-confrontational. But when someone's actions are affecting me in a negative way, or affecting others around me, I can't stay quiet. Either way, I don't usually say something around others. I ask them “is this a bad time to talk?” (works better than “is this a good time to talk?” which is easier to say no to) and then have a seat before chatting. I don't text or email things like this, and phone calls and zoom aren't ideal. In-person, heart-to-heart, as a way to help provide a different perspective to them, and maybe feedback, if they are open to taking it that way.

If you decide to hold the line

A boundary is about what you will do, not about changing them. No debate. No persuading.

“We’re hosting Thanksgiving at our house this year. We’d love to have you.”

“I can’t take that weekend. I’ve covered the last two holidays.”

“We’re inviting everyone. Hope you’ll come.”
