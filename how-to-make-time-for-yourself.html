Make Room · Time for Yourself

How to Make Time for Yourself When Everyone Needs You

one hour that belongs to you

Lauren Fine, MD November 1, 2026 12 min read

You take care of patients, clients, kids, parents, a team, a house. Everyone gets a piece of your day. You get whatever is left, and most days nothing is left.

If you have typed "no time for myself" into a search bar at 11 p.m., you are in good company. You will never find time for yourself. You have to make room for it. This guide shows you how, starting with one hour a week.

Why do I have no time for myself?

Because time for yourself is the only thing on your calendar that nobody else is waiting for. Work has deadlines. Kids have pickup times. Other people ask out loud. You do not ask, so you do not get.

The feeling is common, and it is sharpest for the people carrying the most. In a December 2015 Gallup poll, 48% of U.S. adults said they did not have enough time to do what they wanted. Among working Americans the number was 61%. Among parents of children under 18, it was also 61%.

Researchers call this time poverty: the ongoing sense that there is never enough time. It is a real strain, and it does not get better by waiting for a calm week. The calm week does not come.

Is it selfish to make time for yourself?

No. Time for yourself is maintenance. No physician would tell a patient to skip sleep, skip meals and run on empty for months, then act surprised when something breaks. Yet many of us do exactly that to ourselves and call it dedication.

The people who depend on you get a better version of you when you have had some room to breathe. More patient. More present. Less likely to snap over a lost shoe. Making time for yourself is part of how you keep showing up for them.

Here is the deeper point. Many of us try to make up for a life with no room in it. We plan a big vacation. We buy something nice. Those help for a week. Then we come home to the same calendar. The better fix is to change how the ordinary weeks are built, so you find some joy in them instead of compensating for them later. That is what this page is about.

Why is time for yourself important?

Time for yourself is where you recover, think and remember who you are outside your roles. Without it, the strain of time poverty keeps building, and the people around you feel it too.

You do not need endless free time to feel the benefit. In a 2021 paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, Marissa Sharif, Cassie Mogilner and Hal Hershfield analyzed time-use data from more than 21,000 Americans. Well-being rose with free time and leveled off at about two hours a day. It began to dip after about five hours. Most busy parents are nowhere near five. Some protected time, on a regular basis, goes a long way.

How do I find out where my time goes?

Before you can make room, you need to see what is filling the space. Most of us guess wrong. We remember the big obligations and forget the twenty minutes here and there that disappear into email, errands and the phone.

Start with a time audit. I built a free, ten-minute version called Find Your Hour. It is adapted from the time audit Harvard Business School professor Ashley Whillans uses in her research. It has five steps:

  1. Map a normal day. Pick a regular Tuesday and write down what fills it. Include the scrolling.
  2. Mark each one. M for meaningful, P for pleasant, D for draining. Some things get two letters.
  3. Find the leaks. Circle anything marked only D. Ask: can I stop it, hand it off, or pay to have it done?
  4. Claim one hour. Pick one hour this week you will not give away. Put it on your calendar now.
  5. Decide what it is for. Sleep, a walk, a friend, a book or nothing at all. It only has to be yours.

Get the full Find Your Hour worksheet here. It takes ten minutes. Most people find at least one hour they did not know they had. You can find more free worksheets on the resources page.

How do you find time for yourself as a working mom?

Working mothers carry two full jobs and a mental load that never clocks out. The usual self care advice (a bubble bath, a spa day) misses the point. A bath does not help if you spend it thinking about the permission slip you forgot to sign.

Self care for busy moms has to be built into the week, not squeezed in after it. A few things that help:

  • Schedule it like an appointment. If it is not on the calendar, it is optional, and optional things lose to everyone else's needs.
  • Trade time with your partner. You take Saturday morning, they take Sunday morning. Name it out loud so it is protected.
  • Lower the standard on something. Store-bought cupcakes for the class party. Laundry folded once a week. Pick one place to be good enough instead of perfect.
  • Use the margins on purpose. The fifteen minutes before school pickup can be a podcast you chose, not more email.
  • Let the kids see you take it. They learn what a full life looks like by watching yours.

If the problem is that every request lands on you, the fix starts with boundaries. Read how to set boundaries when you are the one everyone relies on.

How to make time for yourself in harder seasons

The one-hour plan works for most people. Some seasons need a different shape.

As a stay-at-home mom

When your work is at home, it never clocks out, and nobody hands you a lunch break. Schedule your time the way a job would.

  • Use nap time or quiet time for you first, chores second, at least a few days a week.
  • Set a standing swap with another parent. You take their kids one morning, they take yours another.
  • Name a weekend block with your partner. Same time every week, so it does not need renegotiating.
  • Look for drop-off programs. Many gyms, community centers and places of worship offer a few hours of childcare.

As a single mom

With no one at home to hand off to, the trade has to come from outside the house.

  • Trade with another single parent. Alternate Saturday mornings.
  • Use the hours the kids are already covered. One school-day lunch or one practice a week can be yours.
  • Make a family quiet hour. Everyone does their own thing in their own space.
  • Make specific asks. "Can you take them Thursday from four to six?" gets a yes more often than "Let me know if you can help."

As a caregiver

Caring for an aging parent, a partner or a child with extra needs can take every spare hour. You are far from alone. The 2025 Caregiving in the U.S. report from AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving counted 63 million family caregivers, nearly 1 in 4 U.S. adults. Nearly 1 in 4 caregivers said caregiving makes it hard to take care of their own health.

  • Ask about respite care. Adult day programs and in-home respite give you a scheduled break. Your local Area Agency on Aging can point you to options.
  • Book your own appointments first. Your checkup goes on the calendar before the next errand.
  • Build a rotation. Siblings, friends and neighbors each take one specific shift.
  • Take short daily resets. Ten minutes outside, alone, every day.

With a newborn

With a newborn, the goal shrinks. Forget the hour for now. Aim for ten minutes a day that are yours, and count sleep as time for yourself.

  • Treat a shower and a real meal as non-negotiable.
  • Trade one feed or one night shift with a partner or helper, if you can.
  • Say yes to help with meals and laundry, so the time you get is not spent on chores.
  • Step outside for five minutes while someone else holds the baby.

If you feel persistently sad, anxious or unable to cope in the weeks after birth, talk with your doctor. Postpartum depression is common and treatable.

If any of these seasons leaves you carrying the whole household in your head, read how to reduce mental load. If it has tipped into feeling like too much, start with how to stop feeling overwhelmed.

How do I have me time when my day is in pieces?

Sometimes you do have free time. It just comes in scraps. Five minutes in the car line. Ten minutes between meetings. Twenty minutes after the kids are asleep, half of it spent answering texts.

Ashley Whillans calls this time confetti, a term coined by journalist Brigid Schulte. In her book Time Smart (Harvard Business Review Press, 2020), Whillans describes it as little bits of seconds and minutes lost to unproductive multitasking. She points to phones and constant notifications as a main cause. An hour of free time broken up by pings does not feel like an hour.

So aim for time in one piece. Try this:

  • Protect one block. Thirty uninterrupted minutes beats an hour of scraps.
  • Put the phone in another room. Notifications off, door closed, if you can.
  • Batch the small stuff. Answer texts and email at set times instead of all evening long.
  • Pick in advance. Decide what the block is for before it starts, so you do not drift into scrolling.

How to block time for yourself in Outlook

If your days run on a work calendar, put your time there too. In Outlook, create a recurring event, set Show As to Busy, and mark it Private so coworkers see only that you are unavailable. Give it a plain name if you prefer. If your organization uses Microsoft Viva Insights, its focus plan can book focus time on your calendar each day and silence Teams chats while it runs. The same idea works in any calendar app. An event that shows you as busy is harder for anyone, including you, to give away.

Ways to take time for yourself

Time for yourself does not have to be a spa day. Match the idea to the time you actually have.

  • Ten minutes: coffee outside without your phone. A few pages of a novel. A slow stretch.
  • Thirty minutes: a walk alone. A podcast you picked. Cooking something you enjoy because you enjoy it.
  • An hour: an exercise class. Lunch with a friend. A nap with the door closed.
  • A half day: a museum, a hike, a matinee by yourself.

If exercise or cooking is what you want more of, how to make time for healthy habits has a plan for each.

Should I pay to get time back?

If you can afford it, the research says yes. In a 2017 study in PNAS, "Buying time promotes happiness", Whillans and colleagues at Harvard Business School and the University of British Columbia surveyed more than 6,000 adults in the United States, Canada, Denmark and the Netherlands. People who spent money to buy themselves free time reported greater life satisfaction, and that link held even after accounting for income.

They also ran an experiment. Sixty working adults spent $40 on a time-saving purchase one weekend and $40 on a material purchase another weekend. They felt happier after the time-saving purchase.

Most of us do not think this way. In the same paper, only 2% of 98 working adults said they would spend a $40 windfall on something that saved them time. Even among 850 millionaires surveyed, almost half spent nothing to outsource tasks they disliked.

Paying for time can be small. Grocery delivery. A cleaner every other week. A teenager who walks the dog. Then use the time you buy for something that fills you up, not for more work. And if paying is not an option, look at the other two questions from the audit: can you stop it, or can you hand it off?

How do you find time for hobbies?

Hobbies are often the first thing high achievers give up. They feel indulgent. They do not produce anything you can put on a CV. That is part of why they matter. A hobby is one of the few places where you get to be a beginner, with no stakes and no one grading you.

  • Start smaller than you think you should. Twenty minutes of the guitar, one chapter of a novel, one row of knitting.
  • Attach it to an anchor. Read after the kids go to bed. Sketch with your morning coffee. New habits stick better when they ride on old ones. More on that in how to build a habit.
  • Make it social. A class, a walking group or a book club puts it on the calendar and gives you a reason to show up.
  • Make room first. Use the one in, one out rule. Before you add a hobby, name what it replaces.

If you have tried this before and it faded by February, read how to keep New Year's resolutions past the first few weeks. The same rules apply to a hobby.

What if I feel guilty taking time for myself?

Guilt usually means you have been taught that your needs come last. It does not mean you are doing something wrong. Notice the guilt, name it, and take the hour anyway.

For many women, the guilt comes from a long habit of keeping everyone else comfortable. If you find yourself giving your hour away the moment someone asks, read how to stop people pleasing. Then practice one sentence: "I'm not available then." You do not owe a reason. If you want more words, these scripts for saying no work at the office and at home.

It also helps to remember the bigger picture. Work-life balance is not a perfect split. It is a life where the things that matter to you, including you, get real time on the calendar.

Make room

You do not need a free weekend or a quiet month. You need one hour that belongs to you, protected like any other appointment. Do the Find Your Hour audit. Claim the hour. Try it for two weeks and notice what changes.

Frequently asked questions

How do I make time for myself when I'm busy?

Start with a time audit to see where your hours go, then claim one hour a week and put it on your calendar like an appointment. Protect it for two weeks before you add more.

How much time for myself do I need?

There is no magic number. One protected hour a week is a realistic start for most busy people. Uninterrupted time matters more than total minutes, so aim for one solid block instead of scattered scraps.

How can a working mom find time for herself?

Schedule it, trade protected time with a partner, lower the standard on one household task, and outsource what you can afford to. Build self care into the week instead of waiting for leftover time.

Is it selfish to make time for yourself?

No. Time for yourself is maintenance. You show up with more patience and energy for the people who depend on you when you are not running on empty.

How do I find time for hobbies?

Start with twenty minutes, attach it to something you already do, and make it social if you can. Before you add it, decide what it replaces on your calendar.

What is time confetti?

Time confetti is a term coined by journalist Brigid Schulte and used by Harvard Business School professor Ashley Whillans for the small bits of seconds and minutes lost to unproductive multitasking. Phones and notifications break free time into pieces that do not feel restful.

Why is time for yourself important?

It is where you recover and think. In a 2021 study of more than 21,000 Americans, well-being rose with free time and leveled off at about two hours a day, so even modest protected time helps.

How do I block time for myself in Outlook?

Create a recurring event, set Show As to Busy, and mark it Private. If your organization uses Microsoft Viva Insights, its focus plan can book daily focus time and silence Teams chats during it.

How can a caregiver make time for themselves?

Schedule respite care or a rotation of family and friends, book your own health appointments first, make specific requests for help, and take a short break alone every day.
