How to Set Boundaries With Your Boss

frame it around results

Lauren Fine, MD November 15, 2026 6 min read

Your boss is not your family and not your friend, but they shape most of your waking hours. Setting boundaries with a boss feels risky because it is: they control your schedule, your reviews, and sometimes your future.

It is still possible to protect your time and stay respected. The trick is to frame boundaries as how you do your best work, not as what you refuse to do. This page covers how, with word-for-word scripts.

What boundaries with a boss look like

  • Hours. When you are reachable, and when you are not.
  • Workload. What you can take on, and what has to move to make room.
  • Time off. Days off that are actually off.
  • Communication. How you prefer to get requests, and how fast you reply.
  • Respect. No yelling, no public criticism, no personal comments.

How to set boundaries with your boss

  1. Know your priorities. Ask what matters most this quarter. Boundaries are easier to defend when they protect the work your boss cares about.
  2. Frame it around results. "I do my best work when..." lands better than "I won't..."
  3. Be consistent before you announce it. If you reply at 10 p.m. for months, a sudden boundary is a shock. Start by changing your behavior, then name it.
  4. Offer a plan for urgent things. "If something is truly urgent, call me" makes a boundary feel safe.
  5. Make trade-offs visible. Never refuse outright. Ask which priority should move.
  6. Follow through. A boundary you break the first time it is tested teaches your boss it was never real.

Scripts for boundaries with your boss

After-hours messages

  • “I'm offline after 7 so I can be sharp in the morning. If something is urgent, please call.”
  • “I saw this last night and wanted to give it proper attention. Here's my answer.” (Sent at 8 a.m.)

Extra work

  • “I can take this on. If I do, the Q3 report will slip a week. Which would you like first?”
  • “Here is everything on my plate. Which of these should come off to make room?”

Time off

  • “I'll be fully offline Friday. Sam is covering, and I've left notes on the open items.”
  • “I won't be checking email on vacation. I'll catch up the morning I'm back.”

Last-minute requests

  • “I can't get that done by 5 today. I can have it to you by noon tomorrow.”
  • “To do this well I need a day. Can the deadline move?”

Disrespect

  • “I want to hear the feedback. I'd ask that we talk about it privately.”
  • “I'm going to step out and we can continue when things are calmer.”

If you work in medicine

  • “I can't add patients to Thursday's clinic. I can offer two slots next week.”
  • “I've covered the last two holiday weekends. I'm not available this time.”

What the research says

Ask “how” and “what,” not “yes or no.” Negotiator Chris Voss recommends open questions that invite the other person to solve the problem with you. “How would you like me to prioritize this against the Q3 report?” or “What would you like me to move to make room?” You never say no. The trade-off becomes theirs to decide.

Source: Never Split the Difference by former FBI hostage negotiator Chris Voss with Tahl Raz (Harper Business, 2016). More from the Black Swan Group, Voss's company.

Protect your standing by being caring and capable. Organizational psychologist Alison Fragale describes status as the respect other people grant you, and argues it goes to people seen as both caring and capable. Applied to boundaries: frame a limit around doing excellent work for the team, and offer a plan for what matters most. A limit like that signals both.

Source: Likeable Badass: How Women Get the Success They Deserve by Alison Fragale, an organizational psychologist and business school professor at UNC Chapel Hill. Applying it to boundaries is our take, not hers.

How to set boundaries at work without getting fired

Most bosses respond better to a clear boundary than to quiet resentment followed by a resignation. Still, protect yourself:

  • Know your actual obligations. Read your contract, job description, or call schedule. A boundary inside your obligations is safer than one outside them.
  • Lead with performance. Do excellent work on what matters most. It buys room for limits elsewhere.
  • Put agreements in writing. After a conversation, send a short recap.
  • Watch for retaliation. If setting a reasonable limit leads to punishment, document it and talk to HR or an employment attorney.

When your boss keeps pushing

Restate it once, briefly, and offer the trade-off again. If the pattern continues, it may be a workload problem, not a boundary problem. Here is how to say your workload is too much, with an email template. More scripts are in how to say no at work and with family.

If every boundary is met with pushback, that tells you something about the job. Should I quit my job? can help you think it through.

Frequently asked questions

How do I set boundaries with my boss?

Learn what matters most to your boss, frame your boundary around doing your best work, offer a plan for anything truly urgent, and make trade-offs visible instead of refusing outright. Then follow through consistently.

How do I set boundaries at work without getting fired?

Stay inside your actual obligations, lead with strong performance on priorities, put agreements in writing, and document any retaliation. A clear, professional boundary is usually received better than quiet burnout.

How do I tell my boss I won't answer emails after hours?

Say it as a plan: "I'm offline after 7 so I can be sharp in the morning. If something is urgent, please call." Then reply first thing the next morning, every time.

Can I say no to my boss?

Usually the better move is not a flat no but a visible trade-off: "I can take this on. If I do, the report slips a week. Which would you like first?" That lets your boss choose the priority.

What if my boss doesn't respect my boundaries?

Restate it once and offer the trade-off again. If it continues, raise it as a workload issue, document it, and consider HR. Persistent disregard is useful information about whether the job fits.
