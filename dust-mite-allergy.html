Dust Mite Allergy Relief: An Allergist's Bedroom Checklist

fix the room before you blame yourself

Lauren Fine, MD October 14, 2026 6 min read

You wake up stuffy. Your eyes itch before you have had coffee. You sneeze through the first hour of the day and feel fine by lunch. You assume you are run down, or getting a cold that never quite arrives.

If that pattern sounds familiar, look at your bed. Dust mites are one of the most common indoor allergy triggers, and the bed is where they live. The good news is that the fix is mostly physical. You change the room, in the right order, and the room stops working against you.

What a dust mite allergy actually is

Dust mites are microscopic relatives of spiders. They eat the skin flakes we shed and they thrive in warm, humid places. Most people are not reacting to the mites themselves. They are reacting to proteins in mite waste and body fragments, which collect in fabric and get stirred into the air when you move.

That detail explains almost everything about how to treat it. The allergen is heavy. It does not float around the room for hours. It sits in your mattress, pillows, bedding, carpet and upholstered furniture, and it reaches you when you lie down or shake out a blanket.

Dust mite allergy symptoms

  • Stuffy or runny nose, often worse in the morning
  • Sneezing, especially when making the bed
  • Itchy, watery or puffy eyes
  • Postnasal drip and throat clearing
  • Cough or wheezing in people with asthma
  • Itchy skin or eczema flares in some people
  • Poor sleep, and the daytime tiredness that follows it

These symptoms overlap with colds, pollen allergy and other conditions. A pattern that lasts year-round and peaks in bed is a clue, not a diagnosis. An allergist can confirm it with a skin test or blood test.

Why it's worse at night and in the morning

You spend seven or eight hours a night with your face against the place mites are most concentrated. Pillows and mattresses hold warmth and moisture from your body, which is exactly what mites need. If your symptoms are worst at bedtime or on waking, the bed is the first place to look.

The allergist's bedroom checklist

Do these in order. The first three do most of the work. The rest add to them.

1. Encase the mattress and pillows

Put zippered, allergen-proof covers on the mattress, box spring and pillows. A sealed encasement puts a barrier between you and the reservoir. A regular padded mattress protector is not the same thing, even if it is labeled hypoallergenic. If allergy is the reason you are buying, look for a full zippered encasement.

2. Wash bedding hot, every week

Wash sheets, pillowcases and blanket covers weekly in water that is at least 130 degrees Fahrenheit. Hot water kills mites and washes away the allergen. If your fabric can't take that heat, tumble it in a dryer hotter than 130 degrees for at least 15 minutes. Choose bedding you can actually wash that often. 3. Keep humidity under 50 percent

Dust mites do not drink water. They absorb moisture from the air. Keep indoor humidity below 50 percent and they struggle to survive. In a humid climate like South Florida, this is the step that decides whether the rest of the list works. A simple humidity monitor tells you where you stand. A dehumidifier is how you move the number.

4. Vacuum with a sealed HEPA system

Vacuuming removes surface dust, not most of the mites deep in fabric. That is why it comes after the first three steps. A vacuum without a sealed HEPA filter pulls allergen off the floor and blows the fine part back into the air. Use a HEPA vacuum and vacuum weekly. If your allergy is severe, let someone else do it, and stay out of the room for about two hours afterward.

5. Cut down what collects dust

  • Choose hard floors over wall-to-wall carpet in the bedroom when you can.
  • Keep upholstered furniture, throw pillows and extra blankets to a minimum.
  • Wash stuffed animals in hot water, or keep them out of the bed.
  • Swap heavy curtains for washable ones or blinds you can wipe.
  • Dust with a damp cloth so the allergen sticks instead of flying.
  • If you have central air, use a MERV 11 or 12 filter and change it every three months.

What doesn't work well on its own

An air purifier. Mite allergen rides on heavier particles that settle out of the air within minutes. An air purifier does far less for dust mites than for pet dander or pollen. If you also react to those, a true HEPA unit in the bedroom is worth having. It is not a substitute for the steps above.

One change by itself. Studies that tested single measures, like covers alone, found little benefit. The checklist works as a set. Encasements, hot washing and low humidity together are what lower your exposure.

Sprays and powders. Products that promise to kill mites on contact are not a replacement for washing and humidity control.

Dust mite allergy treatment

Changing the bedroom lowers your exposure. Medication treats the symptoms while those changes take effect. Common options include antihistamines and steroid nasal sprays, and some are available over the counter. Ask your doctor or pharmacist which is right for you, especially if you have asthma, are pregnant or take other medicines.

If symptoms continue, see an allergist. Testing confirms whether dust mites are the trigger. For the right person, allergen immunotherapy can reduce the allergy itself over time. It comes as allergy shots or as an FDA-approved tablet that dissolves under the tongue.

Make room

When you feel tired and foggy every morning, it is easy to decide you need more discipline. Sometimes you need a different bedroom. Start with the three steps that matter most. Encase the bed. Wash it hot every week. Get the humidity under 50 percent. Give it a few weeks before you judge the result.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my dust mite allergy worse at night and in the morning?

Your bed is where dust mites live in the highest numbers, and you spend seven or eight hours with your face against it. Symptoms that peak at bedtime or on waking point to the bed first.

What is the fastest way to get dust mite allergy relief?

Put zippered allergen-proof covers on the mattress and pillows, wash bedding weekly in water at least 130 degrees Fahrenheit, and keep indoor humidity under 50 percent. Medication can treat symptoms while those changes take effect.

Does an air purifier help a dust mite allergy?

Not much on its own. Dust mite allergen rides on heavier particles that settle out of the air within minutes, so it stays in bedding, carpet and upholstery. An air purifier helps more with pet dander and pollen.

Can a dust mite allergy make you tired?

It can. Nasal congestion at night disrupts sleep, and poor sleep shows up as daytime fatigue. Treating the congestion and the bedroom often improves both.

Can a dust mite allergy be cured?

Allergen immunotherapy, given as allergy shots or as an FDA-approved tablet under the tongue, can reduce sensitivity over time. An allergist can confirm the allergy with testing and tell you whether you are a candidate.
