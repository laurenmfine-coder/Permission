AI jobs for physicians

what it pays, and what to check first

Lauren Fine, MD November 18, 2026 6 min read

A new kind of side work has opened up for physicians: helping train and test AI models on medical questions. It is remote, it pays by the hour, and it uses what you already know.

It is also new, uneven and easy to misunderstand. Here is what the work is, who is hiring, what it pays and what to check before you sign up.

What the work actually is

  • Writing prompts and cases. You write realistic clinical questions and scenarios that test a model.
  • Grading answers. You score AI responses for accuracy, safety and completeness, often against a rubric.
  • Comparing answers. You see two responses to the same case, pick the better one and explain why.
  • Red-teaming. You try to get the model to give unsafe advice, so the developers can fix it.

None of it involves patient care. It looks a lot like writing and reviewing exam questions, which many physician educators already do.

Who is hiring physicians

The names change quickly. As of November 2026, physicians most often mention Mercor, Handshake AI, Outlier, Snorkel AI, Turing, micro1 and Medcase. Most hire as contractors for specific projects, not as employees.

What it pays

Pay varies by platform, specialty and project. A snapshot from public listings in November 2026:

MercorAbout $115 to $190 an hourSome projects ask for a minimum of 20 hours a week.

Rates come from public job listings in November 2026 and change often. Treat them as a range, not a promise.

What to check before you apply

  • Your contract. Many employment contracts and academic appointments limit outside work. Read yours, and ask if you are unsure.
  • Conflicts of interest. If you sit on committees, write exam items or advise companies, check whether this work needs to be disclosed.
  • Taxes. Contractor income is usually paid without withholding. Plan for it.
  • Privacy. Never paste real patient information into a task, even de-identified.
  • Stability. Projects can end without notice. Do not build your budget on one.
  • Red flags. A legitimate platform never charges you to apply or to get work.

How to apply

Create a profile on one or two platforms, list your specialty and board status clearly, and include teaching or item-writing experience if you have it. Most platforms screen with a short assessment. Expect a wait between being accepted and getting a project.

Is it right for you?

For some physicians, this is a way to earn on their own schedule and stay close to where medicine is heading. For others, it is a sign of something bigger. If you are looking at AI work because you want out of clinical medicine, it is worth asking what you are actually trying to change first.

If you are weighing a bigger change, read non-clinical careers for physicians, or let's talk it through.

Make room

Side work fills time fast. Before you add hours, decide what they are for and what you will stop doing to make room for them.

Frequently asked questions

How much do AI training jobs pay physicians?

Posted rates vary widely by platform, specialty and project. In November 2026, physician listings on Mercor ranged from about $115 to $190 an hour, and Handshake AI listed MD roles at up to $125 an hour. Rates change often.

What do physicians do in AI training jobs?

Write realistic clinical questions and cases, grade AI answers for accuracy and safety, compare two answers and explain which is better, and try to make a model give unsafe advice so it can be fixed.

Do I need a medical license for AI training work?

Most physician projects ask for an MD or DO and residency training. Many prefer board certification or current practice. Requirements differ by project.

Is AI training work for doctors legitimate?

Many physicians do this work through established platforms. Expect contractor status, variable hours and projects that end without much notice. Never pay to apply.

Can I do AI training work while I practice?

Many physicians do it part time. Check your employment contract and conflict-of-interest policy first, and never share patient information.
