How to Build a Habit That Sticks, and How Long It Really Takes

small, repeated, and a little boring

Lauren Fine, MD November 1, 2026 11 min read

You have started this habit before. Maybe more than once. You bought the journal, set the alarm, downloaded the app. It went well for a week. Then a work deadline hit, a kid got sick, and the habit quietly disappeared.

That pattern is common, and it says very little about your discipline. Most habits fail for predictable reasons. They start too big. They have no fixed time or place. And nothing came off your plate to make room for them.

This guide covers what the research says about how long habits take, why the 21-day rule is wrong, and a simple plan for building a habit that lasts when your life is already full.

How long does it take to form a habit?

Longer than you have been told. The best-known study on this question comes from University College London. In 2010, Phillippa Lally and colleagues published a study in the European Journal of Social Psychology that followed 96 volunteers for 12 weeks. Each person picked one new daily behavior, such as drinking water with lunch or going for a run before dinner. Every day, they reported whether they did it and how automatic it felt.

The typical time to reach automaticity was about 66 days. The spread was wide. Some people got there in 18 days. For others, the researchers estimated it would take 254 days. That upper number is a projection, because the study ended at 12 weeks.

Simpler behaviors tended to become automatic faster than harder ones. A glass of water is easier to automate than fifty sit-ups.

So the real answer to "how long does it take to form a habit" is this: about two months for many people, sometimes much longer, and it depends on the habit and the person. If you are on day 30 and it still takes effort, you are not behind. You are in the middle.

Where did the 21-day rule come from?

The 21-day idea traces back to Maxwell Maltz, a plastic surgeon. In his 1960 book Psycho-Cybernetics, he wrote that it took "a minimum of about 21 days" for an old self-image to dissolve and a new one to form. He was describing what he saw in his patients after surgery, and in himself.

Over the following decades, "a minimum of about 21 days" was shortened to "it takes 21 days to form a habit." The word minimum got lost. So did the word about. What was left was a tidy number that self-help books loved.

The problem with the myth is what it does to you on day 22. If the habit still feels hard, you decide you failed. In reality you were never close to the finish line, because the finish line was never at 21 days.

Why can't I stick to habits?

When a client tells me they cannot stick to anything, we look at the habit before we look at them. The same problems come up again and again.

  • It is too big. "Exercise every day" is a goal. A ten-minute walk after dinner is a habit.
  • It has no cue. "Sometime this evening" is not a time. Habits form when the same action happens in the same context, over and over.
  • It depends on motivation. Motivation is high on the first day and low on a rainy Thursday. A habit that needs you to feel inspired will not survive the Thursday.
  • Nothing made room for it. You added something to a full day without taking anything out. Something had to give, and the new habit was the easiest thing to drop.
  • The goal was everything at once. New workout, new diet, new bedtime, new morning routine, all on the same Monday.

Habits matter because so much of life runs on them. In a diary study by Wendy Wood, Jeffrey Quinn and Deborah Kashy (2002), about 43 percent of the behaviors people reported were done almost daily, in the same setting, often while they were thinking about something else. That is the power you are trying to borrow. You want the new behavior to run on autopilot too.

Why do habits matter more than motivation?

Motivation is a feeling. It rises and falls with sleep, stress, weather and how the day went. A habit is tied to a cue instead of a mood. Once it forms, the cue starts the behavior, and you no longer have to talk yourself into it.

That is what the Wood, Quinn and Kashy diary study points to. A large share of daily life runs in the same settings, on autopilot. Motivation is useful for getting started. Habits carry you through the days when motivation does not show up. If exercise is the habit you keep postponing, read how to exercise without motivation.

Habits vs. goals: what is the difference?

A goal is a result you want. A habit is something you do, often without thinking, that moves you toward it. "Run a 5K in the spring" is a goal. "Walk for ten minutes after dinner" is a habit.

You need both. The goal gives you a direction. The habit does the daily work. Goals end when you reach them or give up on them. Habits keep going. That is why many people hit a goal, stop the habit that got them there, and slide back.

A quick test: if you cannot do it today, in a few minutes, it is a goal. Break it down until you find the habit inside it. If you set yearly goals, how to keep New Year's resolutions shows how to turn a resolution into one small daily action. If your list of goals is long enough to feel heavy, start with how to stop feeling overwhelmed and pick one.

How do you build a habit, step by step?

1. Make room first

Before you add anything, decide what it replaces. Twenty minutes of scrolling. A standing call that could be an email. A commitment you agreed to out of guilt. If you cannot see where the time will come from, do a quick time audit with the Find Your Hour worksheet. It shows you where your week goes before you ask it to hold one more thing.

If the thing that has to come out is something you do for other people, that is a boundary question. Start with how to set boundaries, and if every request still feels impossible to turn down, read how to stop people-pleasing.

2. Start smaller than feels useful

Pick one habit. Then shrink it until it feels almost too easy. One page of a book. Five minutes of stretching. One glass of water before your coffee.

Small is the strategy here. You are training the routine, not the result. You can always do more once you start. You cannot keep a habit you dread.

3. Decide exactly when and where

Psychologists call this an implementation intention. It is an if-then plan: "When it is 6:30 a.m. and I am in the kitchen, I will do five minutes of stretching." The research behind it is strong. A 2006 meta-analysis by Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran pooled 94 independent tests and found that forming if-then plans had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals.

Write the plan down. Put it on your calendar. A habit with an address is much harder to skip.

4. Stack it on something you already do

The easiest cue is a routine you already have. More on this below, because it deserves its own section.

5. Make it easy and a little boring

Lay out your walking shoes the night before. Keep the water bottle on your desk. Put the book on your pillow. Every bit of friction you remove is one less decision on a tired day.

And expect it to be dull. I wrote about this in three myths that are costing you money and sleep. Lasting change is small, repeated and a little boring. Boring is often a sign the habit is working.

What is habit stacking, and does it work?

Habit stacking means attaching a new habit to one you already do every day. James Clear popularized the term in his 2018 book Atomic Habits. It builds on the work of Stanford behavior scientist BJ Fogg, whose Tiny Habits method pairs a very small new behavior with an existing routine he calls an anchor.

The formula is simple:

After I [current habit], I will [new habit].

  • After I pour my morning coffee, I will write one line about the day ahead.
  • After I park the car at work, I will take three slow breaths before I go in.
  • After I put the kids to bed, I will put my phone on the charger in the kitchen.
  • After I finish dinner, I will walk around the block.

It works for the same reason implementation intentions work. The old habit becomes the "when." You already do it without thinking, so it reminds you without effort. Choose an anchor that happens at the same time and place every day, and keep the new habit small enough to finish in a few minutes.

How to habit stack in three steps

  1. List your anchors. Write down what you already do every day without fail. Start the coffee. Brush your teeth. Park the car. Sit down to dinner.
  2. Match the anchor to the habit. A morning habit needs a morning anchor. A habit that needs a quiet room should not ride on the school run.
  3. Write the sentence and start tiny. "After I start the coffee, I will drink one glass of water." Add a second link to the chain only after the first one holds.

What happens if you miss a day?

Almost nothing. In the Lally study, missing one opportunity did not materially affect the habit formation process. People who skipped a day picked up where they left off.

Missing many days was a different story. Repeated misses slowed progress. So the practical rule is simple: never miss twice. One missed day is life. Two in a row starts a new pattern.

On a hard day, shrink the habit instead of skipping it. One minute of stretching. One paragraph. The point is to keep the routine alive until tomorrow.

Do habit trackers work?

For many people, yes. Tracking turns a vague sense of "I've been doing okay" into something you can see. A meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin led by Benjamin Harkin pooled 138 studies with nearly 20,000 participants. People who monitored their progress were more likely to reach their goals. The effect was larger when progress was physically recorded or reported to others.

Keep your habit tracker simple:

  • Use paper if you like paper. A calendar on the fridge with a check mark for each day works as well as an app.
  • Track one habit. Ten rows of boxes turns into one more chore.
  • Count the small version. Five minutes on a hard day still earns the check mark.
  • Review weekly. Look for the pattern, not the streak. Which days did you miss, and what got in the way?

A broken streak means nothing more than one missed day. Never miss twice, and keep marking.

Why try it for two weeks?

Here is my one command for anyone who is afraid to change: try it for two weeks.

To be clear, two weeks will not make a habit automatic. The research says that usually takes much longer. Two weeks is a trial period. It is short enough that you can say yes without negotiating with yourself. It is long enough to learn something real.

At the end of two weeks, ask three questions:

  • Did I do it most days?
  • Do I notice any difference, even a small one?
  • What got in the way, and can I fix that?

If the answers are mostly yes, keep going. You are now on the long road toward automatic, and you have data that it fits your life. If the answers are no, change one thing and run another two weeks. A different time. A smaller version. A different anchor.

Many people find that approach useful for the start of a new year too. If that is where you are, read how to keep New Year's resolutions.

What do you do with the habits that didn't stick?

You put them in the compost pile.

A habit you dropped still taught you something. You learned that mornings are too crowded, or that you hate running, or that you need a friend to show up. None of that effort is wasted. It breaks down and feeds the next attempt.

Changing course does not erase what came before. The gym membership you stopped using taught you that you like classes better than machines. The meditation app taught you that two minutes works and twenty does not. Each try makes the next one smarter.

So when a habit falls apart, skip the verdict about your character. Ask what it taught you. Then plant the next thing in better soil.

Make room

A habit that sticks needs space in your day. You will never find that space by accident. You make room for it, on purpose, by deciding what matters more than the thing it replaces.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to form a habit?

Longer than most people expect. In a 2010 University College London study led by Phillippa Lally, the typical time for a daily behavior to become automatic was about 66 days. Individual times ranged from 18 days to an estimated 254 days.

Is the 21-day rule true?

No. The idea traces back to plastic surgeon Maxwell Maltz, who wrote in his 1960 book Psycho-Cybernetics that it took a minimum of about 21 days for an old self-image to change. Over time that became a promise that habits form in 21 days. The research does not support it.

What is habit stacking?

Habit stacking means attaching a new habit to one you already do every day, using the formula: after I do my current habit, I will do my new habit. For example, after I pour my morning coffee, I will write one line in a notebook.

Why can't I stick to habits?

Usually the habit is too big, it has no set time or place, or nothing came off your plate to make room for it. Shrink it, tie it to a cue you already have, and decide what it replaces.

Does missing a day ruin a habit?

No. In the Lally study, missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation. Many missed days did slow progress, so the practical rule is to get back on track the next day.

What is the easiest way to start a new habit?

Pick one small habit, decide exactly when and where you will do it, and try it for two weeks. Two weeks will not make it automatic. It gives you enough information to decide whether to keep going.

What is the difference between a habit and a goal?

A goal is a result you want, like running a 5K. A habit is a small action you repeat, often without thinking, that moves you toward it, like a ten-minute walk after dinner. Goals give direction. Habits do the daily work.

How do you make a habit stick?

Make it small, tie it to a cue you already have, and do it at the same time and place each day. Decide what it replaces so it has room. Track it simply, never miss twice, and expect it to take weeks to months to feel automatic.

Do habit trackers help?

For many people, yes. A meta-analysis of 138 studies in Psychological Bulletin found that monitoring progress made people more likely to reach their goals, especially when they recorded it or shared it with others. A check mark on a paper calendar is enough.
