Is This One Worth It?

How to set boundaries with family without guilt (or a fight)

Lauren Fine, MD Two minutes Fill it in here or print it

We all have that friend, family member, or colleague who pushes until the whole group folds. And the holidays bring this out in full force.

The real question isn't "give in or push back." It's whether you are choosing to let it go, or avoiding the fight because you dread it. Both look the same from the outside. They feel very different inside.

This worksheet helps you tell which one it is. You can type right on this page. Your answers stay in your own browser and are never sent anywhere.

Step one

Name it

A friend, a family member, a colleague, a co-parent
Where the holiday happens, the call schedule, the plan everyone else bends around

Step two

Four questions

Answer each one honestly. There is no score to beat.

Step three

If you decide to hold the line

A boundary is about what you will do, not about changing them. No debate. No persuading.

"We're hosting Thanksgiving at our house this year. We'd love to have you."

"I can't take that weekend. I've covered the last two holidays."

"We're inviting everyone. Hope you'll come."

Expect it to get worse before it gets better. Behavioral psychology calls this an extinction burst. When a tactic stops working, people push harder before they quit. If you expect the spike, you are less likely to cave at the peak.

Don't go first alone. In a group, or a department, everyone waits for someone else to say it. Line up one or two allies before the moment comes.

Questions people ask

Common questions

Why is setting boundaries with family so hard?

Because the old pattern works for someone. When one person always pushes and everyone else folds, the group has learned that folding keeps the peace. A boundary changes that, so expect pushback at first. That spike is a sign the boundary is working, not that it is wrong.

How do I set boundaries with family without starting a fight?

Say what you will do, not what they must do. Skip the debate and the persuading. One or two calm sentences, such as "We're hosting this year. We'd love to have you," leave nothing to argue with. Line up an ally before the moment comes so you are not the only voice.

How do I set boundaries with family without feeling guilty?

Decide first whether this one is worth holding. If it is a preference and only you pay the price, letting it go can be a real choice. If it is a value, if someone else pays, or if it happens every time, holding the line protects the people you love. The four questions on this page help you tell which is which.
