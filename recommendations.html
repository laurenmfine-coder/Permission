Dr. Lauren Fine, Allergist

Allergy. Home.
Comfort. Work.

I am a board certified allergist and immunologist, and the question I get most often has nothing to do with medication. It is some version of: what is actually safe to put on my skin, sleep in, and wash with?

This is that answer, in the order I would actually change things. It starts with what the words on a label are worth, because that is the part nobody explains, and it ends with what I wear to work. Everything here is a recommendation, not a treatment.

The three essentials

Sunscreen. Retinol. Hyaluronic acid.

If you follow me on Instagram, you have heard me say this before: these are the three things worth doing every single day. Sunscreen protects the barrier you already have. Retinol is the one active with real evidence behind it for renewal, but it makes skin more sensitive to sun while you use it. Hyaluronic acid holds the water both of those need so skin is not left raw in the process. Each one covers a gap the other two leave open. Everything else on this page is detail. Start here

Three words on the label. Only one is checkable.

Hypoallergenic

Not regulated

There is no standard behind it and no test a product has to pass. The FDA says plainly that no federal standards or definitions govern the word. It is the term people trust most and the one that tells you least.

Unscented

Often misleading

A product can be unscented and still contain fragrance, added specifically to mask the smell of the other ingredients. If you react to fragrance, this word does not protect you.

Fragrance free

Checkable

It means none was added, and you can confirm it on the ingredient list. This is the term dermatologists tell patients to look for. Together with "allergy tested," it is what I look for too.

A note on how to use this. Nothing here treats a condition. If your skin is genuinely inflamed, or you have eczema or hives that are not controlled, that is a conversation with your own physician, not a shopping list.

What this list does is reduce what your skin has to deal with in the first place. Fewer irritants, less sun, less friction, fewer dust mites. That is unglamorous and it is most of the work.

Change one thing at a time. If everything changes at once and your skin improves, you have learned nothing about which change mattered.

The testing

What "allergy tested" actually means

You will notice one brand appears on this page more than any other, so it is fair to ask why. The answer is not loyalty and nothing here is sponsored. It is that I can describe what their testing involves, and for most of what sits next to it on the shelf I cannot. Here is the part worth understanding, including the limits.

01

"Hypoallergenic" is not a claim anyone has to prove

The FDA is unusually blunt about this. There are no federal standards or definitions governing the term, the agency says, and it "means whatever a particular company wants it to mean." No manufacturer has to submit any substantiation to use it.

The FDA did try. A 1975 regulation would have required comparative testing before a product could carry the word. Two cosmetics companies sued and the D.C. Circuit struck the rule down in 1977. One of those companies was Clinique. I would rather tell you that myself than have you find it somewhere else.

What filled the vacuum is measurable. When researchers analyzed skin care products marketed as hypoallergenic in the United States, 89 percent contained at least one known contact allergen.

02

What a real sensitization test looks like

The protocol behind the phrase "allergy tested" is usually the Human Repeated Insult Patch Test. It runs on roughly 100 to 200 volunteers. Nine occluded patches go on the upper back across three weeks. Then a two week rest. Then a fresh challenge patch on skin that has never seen the product, read over the following days.

The design is deliberate and it is not asking whether the product stings. It is asking a harder question: whether repeated forced exposure can teach an immune system to react to something it previously ignored. That is what sensitization is, and it is permanent once it happens.

03

And what that test cannot tell you

A panel of about 100 people cannot detect a sensitization rate below roughly one percent. Nine occluded exposures are nothing like how anyone actually uses a moisturizer. And in the words of the toxicologist reviewing the method, a negative result "does not mean that sensitization will not occur in the exposed population."

So allergy tested is not a promise. It is a screen that catches strong sensitizers before a product ships. That is worth a great deal. It is not the same thing as safe for everyone, and any brand telling you otherwise is overselling.

04

Why fragrance is the ingredient I care most about

Under US labeling law an entire fragrance mixture can appear on an ingredient list as one word: "Fragrance." Those formulas are protected as trade secrets. So on a scented product, the ingredient list genuinely cannot tell you what you are putting on your face.

That matters because fragrance chemicals are among the most common contact allergens found in clinic. In the most recent North American Contact Dermatitis Group series, 3,056 patients were patch tested. Hydroperoxides of linalool, an oxidized fragrance ingredient, were positive in 10.1 percent of them.

Removing fragrance removes an entire category you have no way to audit. On this whole page, that is the single highest yield decision.

05

What Clinique actually claims, in their words and not mine

Clinique states that every product is allergy tested and 100 percent fragrance free, across skincare and makeup, and free from parabens and phthalates. They describe testing at every stage of development, including the Repeated Insult Patch Test, stability, sensory, clinical, ophthalmologist and safety in use testing, and put the running total at "nearly 6 million test applications to date." The brand was built in 1968 around dermatologist Norman Orentreich's premise that what is left out of a formula matters as much as what goes in. Eye products are additionally ophthalmologist tested and formulated for contact lens wearers.

Those are the company's claims about itself. They are not an independent certification, because no such certification exists, which is the whole problem this page is about. What I can say is that the claims are specific rather than atmospheric, and that they apply to the entire line instead of one hero product. Specific claims are falsifiable. "Gentle" and "for sensitive skin" are not.

One clarification while we are being precise. Clinique funds a dermatology research center at Icahn Mount Sinai. That is philanthropic research funding, not a hospital testing or endorsing products, and it should not be read as one.

None of this replaces patch testing. If your skin is reacting and you do not know to what, that is a diagnosis worth getting, and no ingredient list substitutes for it. What a fragrance free, allergy tested line does is reduce the number of variables you are carrying before you start. The longer version of why I trust this brand is here.

Step seven

What I actually wear to work

Everything above is about lowering what your skin has to deal with. This section is the other half of the day. Fiber and fit still matter once you are dressed for the office, and the same rules apply: smooth fibers, nothing that traps heat, and new pieces washed before the first wear. Where these numbers come from

  • US Food and Drug Administration. "Hypoallergenic" Cosmetics. On the absence of any federal standard behind the term.
  • US Food and Drug Administration. Fragrances in Cosmetics. On "unscented" products containing masking fragrance, and on fragrance mixtures being listed as a single trade-secret ingredient.
  • American Academy of Dermatology. Contact dermatitis: how to treat and prevent it. On choosing "fragrance free" over "unscented."
